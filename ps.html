personal essay - draft 10 (final?) - (steelsmoke)




Hollow howls crawl through the alleyways of Saturn. Former watchful eyes and eager hands lay dormant, their memory flitting 
through the savage wind and carried into the dark sky. Deeper within, numerous blazing hearts pump fuel to a forgotten goddess seething with indignation.

Growing up felt lonely. Even within my own family, I've always seen myself as an outsider. Everything before high school is a blur.
So, to cope with the burden of expectations and pressure to improve and existential doubts about my future and past, I make games!

I craft a golden hook, erect poles on each building’s edge, and drape a wire between them. To properly render the wire, 
I begin researching suspension mechanics: reading articles, copying derivations, and studying examples. Then I discover the catenary.

The catenary curve is a mathematical description of a hanging cable; despite the resemblance, catenaries are distinct from parabolas. Architecture makes heavy use of catenaries-- 
Thomas Jefferson first coined the word when referencing the mechanical equilibrium of arches. Creating the catenary was challenging, but the reward justified the toil. 
In rendering a wire, I learned to see catenaries everywhere: a bracelet dangling off a stranger's wrist, power cables strewn around town, 
or the Christmas lights hanging just outside (my favorites). 

This is why I make games. Creativity plus learning equals friends! I've made friends with every wire, and when I look up at the clouds I see friends too.
Because then I see the Navier-Stokes equations and their role in fluid dynamics; I see the countless hours I spent constructing a smoke simulator; 
and I remember myself-- the love I have for what I learn and create. And all my existential doubts vanish, like smoke drifting to the sky.

But a piece lies missing.
When I optimized my wires with Bézier curves, De Casteljau's algorithm did not invent itself. Teaching happened.
As I instruct my characters to jump a certain height or play certain sounds, they also teach me about two-dimensional kinematics and pitch variance; 
a professor can teach, but so can the lingering sadness upon waking from a dream, fresh rain dripping from trees basking in the sun, or a weathered chain strung
between two posts. When I connect with my teachers, they tether me to reality. Because hidden within every lesson is the implicit secret: we are all sisters in stardust.
Whenever I spot a catenary or see the clouds, I remember this, and feel less alone.

The dance between teachers and students gives life meaning. So I will be a dutiful student, and teach my best.
